# Time-Frequency Analysis: Variational Generalized Nonlinear Modal Decomposition Algorithm and Its Applications [with MATLAB Code]

In recent years, the variational signal decomposition methods such as Adaptive Linear Frequency Modulation (ACMD) and Generalized Dispersion Mode Decomposition (GDMD) have attracted widespread attention in various fields. However, these methods struggle to simultaneously separate chirp components and dispersion components. This paper proposes a Variational General Nonlinear Mode Decomposition (VGNMD) framework to address this issue. VGNMD first introduces the Adaptive Time-Frequency Fusion and Clustering (ATFFC) method to enhance the noise resistance and resolution of the time-frequency distribution (TFD) of signals in noisy environments, accurately capturing the TFD of each mode. Subsequently, based on the discrimination criteria for mode types, it establishes the T-F (Time-Frequency) ridges of modes that are either chirp modes or dispersion modes. Finally, using these T-F ridges as initial instantaneous frequencies (IF) or initial group delays (GD), the method employs a variational optimization algorithm to precisely reconstruct modes and refine their IF or GD. Simulation examples and practical applications in bat echolocation signal analysis and railway wheel rail fault diagnosis are considered to demonstrate the effectiveness of VGNMD. The results indicate that this method can accurately extract both chirp and dispersion modes, making it suitable for analyzing nonlinear signals with discontinuous T-F patterns.
Introduction: The state monitoring and fault diagnosis of mechanical equipment are of great significance for ensuring its safe and stable operation [1-5]. Signal decomposition, by breaking down signals into a series of sub-signal components with clear physical meanings, can suppress environmental noise and interference components in mechanical vibration signals [6, 7]. As the fault diagnosis of mechanical equipment evolves towards finer granularity, signal decomposition plays an increasingly important role in weak fault diagnosis and quantitative identification [8]. However, for large mechanical systems such as rail vehicles, complex operating environments (multiple source excitations, time-varying conditions, etc.) often result in state monitoring signals exhibiting characteristics of multimodal mixing, complex non-stationary modulation, and strong noise interference [9, 10]. Specifically, due to the coupling effects of parts in mechanical systems, the vibration signal of one part may contain multiple components from multiple parts, or even have two classes of harmonics (i.e., linear frequency modulation modes with varying instantaneous frequency (IF)) and pulse-like components (i.e., dispersive modes with varying group delay (GD)). Accurately separating these modes and extracting their IF and GD is a very challenging and practical task [13, 14].
The majority of existing signal decomposition methods primarily focus on the separation of certain types of components (i.e., chirp modes or dispersion modes). Chirp modes typically carry important information about the type and extent of machine failures [15]. Many decomposition methods, such as Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) [16], Local Mean Decomposition [17], Empirical Wavelet Transform [18], and Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) [19], are used to separate chirp modes. These methods have attracted widespread attention in various fields due to their unique advantages, such as high adaptability (e.g., EMD) [20] and strong noise robustness (e.g., VMD) [21]. However, most of these methods are limited to separating well-defined narrowband modes in the frequency domain [22]. To effectively analyze broadband non-stationary signals, a variational nonlinear chirp mode decomposition based on optimal demodulation algorithms (VNCMD) [23] has been recently proposed. Unlike the frequency domain filtering groups of VMD [24], VNCMD is essentially a time-frequency (TF) domain filtering group with its center frequency estimated as the IF, capable of decomposing chirp signals with very close or even crossing modes. Additionally, Adaptive Linear Frequency Modulation Mode Decomposition (ACMD) [25] and Data-Driven ACMD (DD-ACMD) [26] have been proposed to enhance the adaptability of VNCMD. Note that although the above variational signal decomposition methods gradually meet the requirements for high adaptability and good noise robustness when analyzing non-stationary signals, they are not suitable for dispersion signal analysis because the chirp mode model cannot represent the dispersion effect.
Frequency dispersion modes are commonly used for source localization and system identification. For dispersion signal decomposition, the Dispersion Compensation Method (DCM) can effectively reduce dispersion effects and has attracted great interest [27]. The method compresses the dispersion waveform with varying GD into a time pulse with constant GD, which can be extracted through a rectangular time window. Inspired by DCM and ACMD, the Generalized Dispersion Mode Decomposition (GDMD) method is developed to handle overlapping or intersecting dispersion modes [28]. This method transforms the pattern decomposition problem into an optimal dispersion compensation problem and solves it through a joint estimation algorithm. Essentially, the best dispersion compensation is a frequency domain dual of the best demodulation algorithm in VNCMD and ACMD. Due to its excellent decomposition capability and noise robustness, GDMD is widely used in structural health monitoring and multi-channel signal decomposition [29]. It is noteworthy that these variational signal decomposition methods extract signal patterns through variational optimization algorithms, which require initialization with an IF or GD. Specifically, ACMD obtains the initial IF through Hilbert transform to drive the variational optimization algorithm to extract chirp modes in the time domain. GDMD obtains the initial GD by extracting TFridges to drive the variational optimization algorithm in the frequency domain to extract dispersion modes. However, due to the limitations of dispersion mode model, these methods are difficult to simultaneously extract dispersion and chirp modes. Furthermore, signals in real life may have certain discontinuous components that exist only within a limited duration and bandwidth of the reference interval. Most of the above methods define modal models over the entire reference interval, so they cannot extract these discontinuous components.
Different from the aforementioned signal decomposition methods, Time-Frequency Analysis (TFA) can simultaneously characterize chirp and dispersion features in the Time-Frequency domain. However, due to the limitations of the Heisenberg-Gabor inequality, traditional TFA methods often result in poor energy concentration in their time-frequency distributions. To enhance the resolution of TFD, researchers have developed numerous post-processing techniques based on TFA re-distribution, such as Synchronous Compression Transform (SST) [30] and its improved versions [31-33]. Unfortunately, these methods only move the TFA coefficients along one direction to maintain signal reconstruction capability, thus failing to simultaneously improve both time and frequency resolutions. To address these issues, the Time-Frequency Multi-Compression Transform (TFMST) was proposed based on the "divide and conquer" strategy, which not only effectively characterizes the mixed signals of harmonic and pulse components but also possesses the ability to reconstruct the signal. Additionally, TFA fusion is an effective technique for improving TFD resolution by integrating different parameters of TFD. It is worth noting that most of the aforementioned methods focus on obtaining high-resolution TFDs without providing an effective strategy for separating chirp components and dispersion components simultaneously.
As mentioned earlier, extracting chirp and dispersion modes from nonlinear signals simultaneously is a challenging task. Inspired by the TF fusion technique and the Variational Signal Decomposition methods ACMD and GDMD, we propose a Variational Generalized Nonlinear Mode Decomposition (VGNMD) framework for adaptively classifying modes and further precisely extracting these modes along with their IF or GD. Unlike ACMD and GDMD, VGNMD can adaptively obtain the type of each mode and its time-frequency profile as initial parameters to drive the variational optimization algorithm, thereby extracting chirp and dispersion modes simultaneously from complex non-stationary signals. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: Firstly, an Adaptive TF Fusion and Clustering (ATFFC) scheme is proposed to enhance TF resolution, eliminate noise interference, and further obtain high-quality TFDs for various modes. Secondly, a mode type discrimination criterion is introduced based on the TF profile of the mode to categorize it into chirp and dispersion modes. These modes' support intervals are determined by the non-zero values in the TF profile. Finally, under the driving of these TF profiles, the variational optimization algorithm precisely extracts the modes along with their IF or GD. Additionally, the reconstructed high-resolution TFDs using these modes and IF/GD are provided in Section 4.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we introduce the generalized nonlinear signal model and review the variational optimization algorithms in ACMD and GDMD. The proposed VGNMD framework is detailed in Section 3. In Section 4, we provide some simulations and actual signals to test the performance of VGNMD. We conclude our paper in Section 5.

Figure 11 shows the reconstruction results of noise signals through different methods. It can be seen that due to strong noise interference, EMD cannot correctly extract signals. Although VMD is set as a relatively accurate initial center frequency (200 Hz), it cannot obtain acceptable reconstruction results due to narrow band limitations. The reconstruction results of ACMD do not converge to the correct results within 2.6 ~ 4 seconds because the initial IF at this time is far from the true IF.
